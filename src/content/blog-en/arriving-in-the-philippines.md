**Three things decide how a trip to the Philippines goes, and none of them is the visa.** A free form that is routinely sold, an extension with a published price, and a connection nobody leaves enough time for.

## 1. eTravel — compulsory, free, asked for twice

The eTravel registration is **compulsory** and **free**. It produces a QR code, and that code is asked for **twice**: by the airline at check-in, and again on arrival.

Both halves matter. People who registered but did not save the code get stopped at the first one, in a queue, with a flight boarding.

**It is also one of the most resold formalities in Asia.** Intermediary sites charge for a government form that takes five minutes, and they buy the search results above the official portal. Reach it from your own government's Philippines travel advice page rather than from a search result — the first result is first because someone paid for it.

The same economy has grown around [Thailand's arrival card and the Korean K-ETA](/en/blog/the-fees-that-are-not-the-visa), and the defence is identical in all three cases.

## 2. The extension — 29 days, and a published price

A tourist stay in the Philippines is extendable. **A 29-day extension takes a typical stay to 59 days in total, for about 3 030 PHP**, at a Bureau of Immigration office.

The fee being published is the useful part, because extensions here are a known point of friction: fixers offer to "handle it", and the only defence is walking in knowing the number.

**The length you start from belongs to your passport**, not to the Philippines in the abstract. The [Philippines page](/en/philippines) gives yours, with the government source and the month it was read. [We costed extensions across the region](/en/blog/extending-your-stay-southeast-asia) if you are comparing.

## 3. The three-hour rule

This is the one that breaks trips.

The Philippines is an archipelago, and almost every itinerary involves internal flights or ferries. **Leave three hours minimum between two internal flights**, and more if the second is the last of the day.

Two reasons, and they compound:

**Delays are structural, not exceptional.** Particularly from June to November, when the typhoon season overlaps the wettest months.

**Two separately booked tickets protect you from nothing.** Miss the connection and no carrier owes you the next seat. You buy it again, and you pay for the night.

The same applies to ferries, with an extra caveat: they cancel for weather without notice, and there is no rebooking desk. [The regional transport guide](/en/guides/getting-around-asia) has the checks worth doing before boarding one.

## The order to do it in

1. **Check the entry rule for your passport** — the [Philippines page](/en/philippines) gives it with its source and date.
2. **Register on eTravel** before you fly, and save the QR code somewhere you can reach without a connection.
3. **Build the itinerary with three-hour connections**, or with single tickets covering the whole journey.
4. **Leave one spare day** before your international flight home. On an archipelago in the wet season, it is not caution; it is the difference between an inconvenience and a missed flight.

## What not to do

**Pay anyone for eTravel.** It is free, on `immigration.gov.ph`.

**Book a 90-minute internal connection** because the schedule says it works. The schedule is not the problem.

**Plan to extend without checking the office.** Extensions are done at Bureau of Immigration offices, not at every town hall, and opening hours are not universal.

**Fly home the same day you fly in from an island.** It is the single most common way a Philippine itinerary goes wrong, and one spare day removes it entirely.
