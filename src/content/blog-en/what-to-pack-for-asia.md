The contents of a bag correct themselves within three days on the ground. The size of the bag does not — it is the one genuinely irreversible decision.

## The rule about volume

**40 to 45 litres**, for three weeks as for three months. This is not minimalist showmanship: it is mechanical. A 70-litre bag fills because it holds 70 litres, and you will carry the difference every day.

Under 40 litres you make cabin baggage on most Asian carriers. On a route with two or three internal flights, that is an hour saved each time and no risk of a lost bag.

**Target weight: 8 to 10 kg**, packed, water included. Beyond that, every station staircase becomes a decision.

Backpack or suitcase? Backpack as soon as there are broken pavements, boats or multiple stops — which is to say everywhere in Southeast Asia. A wheeled case if you are staying in the big cities of Japan or Korea, where the surfaces are perfect.

## Clothes: five days, no more

A load of laundry costs the equivalent of 1 to 3 € a kilo in most of Southeast Asia, and you get it back the next day. Packing two weeks of clothes means carrying five extra kilos to save six euros.

**What a wardrobe that works looks like:**

- 4 to 5 light tops in a fabric that dries fast — heavy cotton never dries at 85 % humidity
- 2 pairs of light trousers, one of them covering the ankles for temples and mosquito evenings
- 1 pair of shorts or a skirt
- 1 properly covering outfit: shoulders and knees, required at many religious sites and enforced
- 5 sets of underwear, 3 pairs of socks
- 1 swimming costume
- 1 fleece or thin jumper — air-conditioned buses, night trains and the northern mountains are cold
- 1 light waterproof shell, not an umbrella

**Shoes: two pairs, never three.** One closed, comfortable walking pair and one pair of sandals that tolerates water. Shoes are the heaviest thing you carry and the least worn.

## Electronics

- Phone, cable, charger
- **A 10,000 mAh power bank** — the one accessory nobody regrets the weight of. It is banned from the hold: it must travel in the cabin.
- **A universal adaptor**: Asia mixes types A, C, G and I between countries, sometimes within the same hotel
- Headphones
- An e-reader if you read — lighter than two books, and useful across twelve hours of bus

**What you do not need:** a laptop if you are not working, an SLR if you do not already photograph regularly at home, a drone. That last one deserves a sentence: in Vietnam, flying a drone requires authorisation from civil aviation **and** from the Ministry of Defence, whatever its weight — including under 250 grams. Customs can hold or confiscate it on entry if you do not have it. Indonesia requires a permit from its civil aviation directorate, applied for a week in advance. Packing a drone just in case therefore means packing an object you will not be able to use and may lose on arrival.

## Documents and money

This is the part where a mistake is expensive.

- A passport valid **six months beyond your return date**, with two blank pages
- E-visas printed in duplicate — many land border posts cannot verify a document on a phone
- Your insurance certificate, with the emergency number written out in full
- Your driving licence **and an international driving permit** if you intend to ride or drive
- Two bank cards, from two different banks, kept in two different places
- 100 to 200 € in cash, in euros or US dollars, in clean notes

**The reflex that saves a trip:** photograph everything and email the set to yourself before you leave. The day your bag disappears, that is the difference between an annoyance and a disaster.

Entry requirements themselves belong to your passport rather than to the country — each [country page](/en/vietnam) gives the rule for yours, with the government source and the month it was read.

## The medical kit

Your own prescriptions in sufficient quantity, in their original packaging with the prescription. The rest — painkiller, antiseptic, dressings, rehydration salts, tropical-strength mosquito repellent, high-factor sunscreen — is built with your doctor rather than from a list.

## The six things that earn their place

1. **A packable 15-litre daypack.** You will not be hauling the big bag around town.
2. **A filter bottle.** Tap water is not drinkable across most of Southeast Asia; over three weeks it saves about sixty plastic bottles and pays for itself comfortably.
3. **A padlock.** For hostel lockers and for closing the bag in bus holds.
4. **A sarong or large scarf.** Beach towel, blanket on an air-conditioned bus, shoulder cover at a temple, spare sheet. It is the most versatile object in a bag.
5. **Earplugs.** The horns start at six, the cockerels earlier.
6. **Three dry bags.** For electronics on boats, for dirty laundry, for documents.

## What everyone carries for nothing

- **A bath towel.** Every kind of accommodation provides one, except some dorms — and a sarong covers it.
- **Full-size shower gel and shampoo.** Available everywhere, cheaper, and heavy.
- **Technical hiking trousers**, unless you are actually trekking. They are hot and they go unused.
- **A paper guidebook.** A kilo, out of date on publication.
- **Clothes "for going out".** Nobody dresses for dinner in Southeast Asia.
- **Large quantities of over-the-counter medicine.** Asian pharmacies are well stocked and very accessible.

## The test before you leave

Pack the bag three days early. Carry it, loaded, for twenty minutes of walking, stairs included.

If it is unpleasant for twenty minutes at home, it will be unbearable for three weeks at 33 °C with 85 % humidity. Take out 20 % of the contents: in almost every case, you will never notice what is missing.
