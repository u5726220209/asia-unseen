Received wisdom says Asia is complicated with children. For most of the countries on this site that is wrong, for a simple reason: across much of Asia children are **welcome everywhere**, including in the restaurants, transport and shops where Europe makes them feel they are in the way.

What genuinely changes comes down to three things: the pace, health, and the choice of stops.

## Pace, the only real adjustment

**One stop per three to four days.** That is the rule everything else follows from. Over a fortnight, four stops at most — where a couple without children would do six or seven.

The limiting factor is not the children's stamina; it is accumulation. A four-hour journey goes perfectly well. Three four-hour journeys in five days do not.

**Two practical consequences:**

- Build in half a day with no plan every other day. A pool, a park, a market with no objective. It is what makes the second week possible.
- Do the sightseeing in the morning. Between noon and four it is 33 °C and nobody wants anything — which is true of the adults too.

## Which countries, in what order

**The simplest: Thailand.** Short distances, well-worn infrastructure, hotels with pools at reasonable prices, excellent international hospitals, and a culture that adores children. The obvious choice for a first family trip. [Where to stay in Bangkok](/en/blog/where-to-stay-bangkok) matters more with children than without: the criterion is minutes on foot from a station.

**The most comfortable: Japan.** Cleanliness, complete safety, utterly reliable transport, and a density of activities children enjoy without being designed for them. The only obstacle is the budget — [fifteen days costed out](/en/blog/japan-15-days-without-jr-pass) gives the order of magnitude.

**Also excellent: South Korea.** The same qualities as Japan, 30 % cheaper, and a strong family culture in public space.

**Possible with well-chosen stops: Vietnam and Indonesia.** Journeys are longer and roads harder. Workable, provided you limit the number of stops and favour private transfers over local buses.

**Leave for later: Laos, rural Cambodia, multi-island Philippines.** Not for safety reasons, but because the logistics — roads, ferries, distance to medical facilities — add a load you can happily do without as a family.

## Health: anticipate more, not differently

This is the point not to improvise. Vaccination and prevention recommendations differ for young children, and some compounds that are routine for adults are not suitable for them.

**The process is the same as for an adult, with more margin:** an appointment eight weeks before departure, with your GP or a travel health clinic, carrying the precise itinerary.

Three practical points that come up repeatedly:

- **Heat and hydration** are the real daily subject, far more than tropical disease. A child dehydrates faster and signals it less well.
- **Mosquito protection** must suit the age: adult formulations are not all appropriate.
- **Insurance** must cover every member of the family, with costs advanced rather than reimbursed later.

## Accommodation

Two adjustments that change the quality of the stay:

**Prefer apartments to hotels** beyond a week. A kitchen, a washing machine and two separate rooms are worth far more than room service.

**Check the floor and the noise.** A first-floor room on a busy street in Hanoi or Bangkok is a sleepless night. Read the last twenty reviews looking for the word "noise".

Hotels with a pool cost 10 to 20 € more a night in Southeast Asia. On a family trip, that is probably the best use of those 20 €.

## Transport

**Internal flights rather than night buses.** A night bus with children is a bad memory for everybody. A 45 € internal flight that replaces twelve hours of road justifies itself far faster than it would on an adult trip.

**Private transfers rather than local transport** over long distances. A car with a driver for 200 km costs 40 to 70 € in Southeast Asia, and lets you stop when you need to.

**Ride-hailing in cities.** Grab, Gojek, Kakao T: the price is fixed in advance, with no negotiating on the pavement with two tired children.

**The point not to compromise on:** the scooter, ubiquitous in Southeast Asia, is not for riding with a child. No suitable helmet, no insurance cover, no margin for error.

## What goes better than in Europe

**The food.** Rice, noodles, skewers, fruit: the staple diet of Southeast Asia suits children very well, and unspiced dishes exist everywhere — you only have to ask. Street stalls cook in front of you, which reassures as much as it entertains.

**The welcome.** In most countries in the region, a child crying in a restaurant does not draw irritated looks. Staff will often pick the child up. It is disconcerting the first time, and a real comfort afterwards.

**The cost.** Many sites are free or reduced for children, and the food budget of a family of four in Southeast Asia stays below that of two adults in Europe.

## A model itinerary: two weeks in Thailand

- **Days 1–4 — Bangkok.** The klongs by boat, Lumphini park, one museum a day at most.
- **Days 5–9 — Chiang Mai.** A family cookery class, markets, an elephant sanctuary **with no contact and no riding** — the distinction matters.
- **Days 10–14 — an island in the gulf.** Koh Samui or Koh Lanta depending on the season. Five days in one place, with no programme.

Three stops, one internal flight, and a second week that repairs the first. That is the format that works.
