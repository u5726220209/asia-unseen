The standard advice is to come between November and March. It is not wrong — it is dry and it is comfortable. But at Angkor specifically the rainy season does better, for three concrete reasons.

## What the rain actually changes

**The moats are full.** Angkor Wat was built to be reflected in water. From January to April the basins are low or dry, and the sunrise reflection — the photograph hundreds of people get up at 4:30 for — simply does not exist. In the rainy season it is there.

**The stone is dark.** Wet sandstone turns from pale grey to deep brown, and the bas-reliefs take on a depth that February's hard light flattens completely. Ta Prohm in fine rain, with the strangler figs dripping, finally looks like the thing you came for.

**There are a third as many people.** It is the most obvious argument and the most decisive one. The Bayon at nine in the morning in February is a queue. The Bayon at nine in the morning in September is a temple.

## What it costs in comfort

Let us be honest about the other side.

You will get caught in a downpour. Usually one, late in the afternoon, between thirty minutes and two hours, and heavy. The rest of the day is overcast or clear. This is not continuous English drizzle — it is a tropical storm, and you deal with it by getting under something.

Humidity sits around 85 %, which makes 30 °C harder work than February's 33 °C. The dirt tracks to the outlying temples turn to mud, which rules out cycling the grand circuit.

**The real constraint is unpredictability.** You cannot plan a sunrise for a given date. Over three days you will probably get one good one.

## The three days, in the right order

The classic mistake is to start with Angkor Wat. Everyone does, which is exactly why you should not.

**Day 1 — the outlying temples.** Banteay Srei, 35 km out, in small pink sandstone, carrying the finest carving on the whole site. Then Banteay Samré. You begin with sculpture rather than scale, and you see Angkor Wat after you have learned how to look.

**Day 2 — the small circuit, backwards.** Almost everyone runs it clockwise, starting at Angkor Wat for sunrise. Run it the other way, starting at Ta Prohm as it opens: you will have it nearly to yourself while the coaches are still parked at Angkor Wat. Bayon mid-morning, Angkor Wat late afternoon.

**Day 3 — the grand circuit, or Koh Ker.** Preah Khan, Neak Pean, Ta Som: the grand circuit is markedly quieter. The alternative, if you have the whole day, is Koh Ker and Beng Mealea, two hours out, nearly deserted, with a seven-tier pyramid nobody puts on a poster.

## The practical part

**The pass is sold at the official office only**, at the visitor centre east of Siem Reap. It is personal, with a photograph taken there and then. A three-day pass is valid across ten consecutive days, which lets you take a rest day in the middle — recommended.

**Hire a tuk-tuk driver by the day, not by the trip.** The equivalent of 15 to 25 euros depending on the circuit. A good driver knows the order of visit that dodges the groups, and will be waiting at the right exit while you walk through the temple.

**Bring something that covers shoulders and knees.** Some levels of Angkor Wat are closed to anyone dressed too briefly, and it is enforced.

**Carry more water than you think.** Three litres per person per day is not excessive at 85 % humidity.

## The full sum

For four days in Siem Reap in the rainy season:

| Item | Cost |
| --- | --- |
| Angkor pass — one day | 37 USD |
| Angkor pass — three days | 62 USD |
| Angkor pass — seven days | 72 USD |
| Tuk-tuk, three days | 45–70 € |
| Guide, one day | 30–60 € |
| Accommodation, low season | 15–35 € a night |
| Food | 12–20 € a day |

**The one-day guide is the best money spent on the trip.** Angkor without explanation stays beautiful stone; with someone reading a bas-relief to you it becomes a five-century story. Take the guide on the second day, once you have seen enough to have questions.
