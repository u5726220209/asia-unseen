**Kyoto is twice as spread out as it looks, and its subway has two lines.** Those two facts explain why the most common piece of advice — stay near the station and walk everywhere — is the one that costs the most time.

## The three areas that work

### Karasuma-Shijo, the centre

**Who it suits:** almost everyone, and particularly stays of three to five nights.

It sits at the crossing of both subway lines, most of the city is walkable from it, and the bus network starts here rather than ending here.

**The decisive argument:** it is the most liveable district in the evening. Kyoto goes to bed early and many areas are deserted after 21:00; here you can eat late and walk home.

### Gion and Higashiyama

**Who it suits:** people who want to step out of the hotel straight into the scenery, and who will accept getting up early.

**The thing that changes everything:** these districts are saturated between 10:00 and 17:00. Sleeping there buys one real advantage — walking Higashiyama at seven in the morning, when there is nobody. If you do not intend to get up early, that advantage disappears and only the price is left.

### Arashiyama and the west

**Who it suits:** a second visit to Kyoto, or the last stop of a long trip. Allow 25 to 40 minutes to reach the centre — fine over a week, tiresome over three nights.

## The case of the station

**It is justified** if your stay is short and points outwards: day trips to Nara, Osaka, Himeji or Hiroshima, a late arrival, an early departure. Being on the rail hub saves time every day.

**It is not justified** for visiting Kyoto itself. The district is functional, without atmosphere, and every day begins with a journey.

## What nobody says about getting around

Kyoto runs on buses far more than on its subway, and its buses are slow at peak hours. That is the real reason distance matters here more than it does in Tokyo.

If your itinerary includes long legs by train, the sum of whether a rail pass pays for itself has changed — [we did it leg by leg](/en/blog/jr-pass-worth-it), and for most two-week itineraries the answer is now no.

## One thing to sort out before you book

How long you may stay. Japan grants 90 days visa-free to many nationalities, on arrival — but the rule belongs to your passport, not to the country in the abstract. The [Japan page](/en/japon) gives the rule for yours, with its source and the month it was read.

## What to remember

- **Karasuma-Shijo** unless you have a reason not to.
- **Gion only if you will get up early** — otherwise you pay for a view you will share with everyone.
- **The station for a hub, not for a stay.**
- **Cherry blossom and November maples**: book months ahead or accept double the price.
