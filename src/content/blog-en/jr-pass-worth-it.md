The JR Pass used to be obvious: you bought it without thinking, and it paid for itself. That is no longer true. After several price rises, the break-even point has moved far enough that most classic itineraries no longer reach it.

The good news: the sum takes ten minutes, and it is mechanical.

## What it costs

Prices shown in summer 2026, ordinary class:

| Duration | Current price | From 1 October 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 7 days | ≈ ¥50,000 | ≈ ¥53,000 |
| 14 days | ≈ ¥80,000 | ≈ ¥84,000 |
| 21 days | ≈ ¥100,000 | ≈ ¥105,000 |

The October 2026 rise applies to sales through overseas agents. The official sales site keeps current prices for a limited period — a detail worth a few thousand yen, and reason enough to compare where you buy.

**Check the price of the day** on [the official Japan Rail Pass site](https://japanrailpass.net/): it is the only one that counts, and resellers show figures that diverge.

## The method, in three steps

### 1. List your long-distance legs

Only the ones linking two cities. Metros, city buses and most local journeys do not count — and a good number are not covered by the pass anyway.

A typical two-week itinerary has four to six such legs. Write them down.

### 2. Price each leg separately

For an order of magnitude, one way on the busiest shinkansen routes:

| Route | Order of magnitude |
| --- | --- |
| Tokyo → Kyoto | ≈ ¥14,000 |
| Tokyo → Osaka | ≈ ¥14,500 |
| Kyoto → Hiroshima | ≈ ¥11,000 |
| Tokyo → Kanazawa | ≈ ¥14,000 |
| Tokyo → Sendai | ≈ ¥11,000 |

These are indicative and vary with booking, class and service. Use a fare calculator to get the exact figure for **your** legs — this is the step everyone skips, and it is the one that decides.

### 3. Compare, and remember what is not covered

Add it up. If the total beats the pass, buy the pass. Otherwise buy tickets individually.

Two items regularly distort the sum against the pass:

- **Nozomi and Mizuho** — the fastest shinkansen — need a specific supplement. Without it you travel on Hikari or Sakura: twenty to thirty minutes longer, but very frequent departures.
- **Some private lines** are not covered at all. The Takayama line, several mountain lines and many regional buses are paid separately.

## The verdict on five common itineraries

| Itinerary | Length | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tokyo – Kyoto – Tokyo | 7 days | **No.** Two legs are nowhere near enough |
| Tokyo – Kyoto – Hiroshima – Tokyo | 7 days | **Borderline.** Worth pricing exactly; the gap is small |
| Tokyo – Alps – Kanazawa – Kyoto – Tokyo | 14 days | **No.** Part of the route is not covered |
| Tokyo – Kyoto – Hiroshima – Kyushu – Tokyo | 14 days | **Yes**, clearly |
| Full loop including Hokkaido and Kyushu | 21 days | **Yes**, without hesitation |

## The option most people miss: regional passes

This is the real answer for most trips.

Each JR group company sells a pass limited to its own area: JR East, JR West, JR Central, Hokkaido, Shikoku, Kyushu. They cost a fraction of the national pass and often cover exactly what you are going to do.

**Two worked examples:**

- A trip concentrated on Kansai — Kyoto, Osaka, Nara, Himeji — has no need of the national pass at all. A JR West regional pass covers it for far less.
- A Tokyo-plus-the-north trip is better served by a JR East pass than by the national one, which charges you for covering areas you will never see.

Do that sum **before** the national one. In half of all cases, it makes the question moot.

## The non-financial argument, and what it is actually worth

The pass has one advantage the sum does not capture: flexibility. You board any train, without booking, without thinking. You change plan on the morning itself.

**That flexibility has real value if your trip is deliberately improvised.** It has none if your itinerary is fixed, which describes most two-week trips.

Be honest with yourself on this point: it is the argument that sells the pass to people who will never use it that way.

## One thing this page cannot tell you

How long you may stay. Japan grants 90 days visa-free to many nationalities on arrival, but the rule belongs to your passport, not to the country in the abstract. The [Japan page](/en/japon) gives the rule for yours, with the government source it came from and the month it was read.

## What to do, concretely

1. Write down your long-distance legs — there are fewer than you think.
2. Price them one by one on a fare calculator.
3. Compare first against a **regional pass**, and only then against the national one.
4. If the gap is under 10 %, buy individual tickets: you keep the Nozomi option and avoid the uncovered sections.
