Transport is where travellers lose the most time — not because Asia is badly served, but because the map lies. Two hundred kilometres of Vietnamese plain is four hours. Two hundred kilometres of Lao mountain is seven.

## The eight-hour rule

Beyond eight hours of daytime overland travel, always compare with flying. A 40 € internal flight that replaces twelve hours of bus hands you back a whole day of the trip — and a day on the ground is worth roughly what you spend per day.

Two exceptions, and they matter.

**The journey is the trip.** The slow boat down the Mekong, the Ha Giang loop by motorbike, a Thai night train: these are experiences, not transfers. Treating them as lost time misses the point entirely.

**The overnight leg.** A night bus or night train saves the day and the hotel night at once. Over 8 to 12 hours it is almost always the better sum.

## Trains, country by country

**Japan and South Korea.** The best network in the world, punctual to the minute. In Japan, do the JR Pass sum rather than buying one by reflex: since the fare revision it no longer pays on most classic routes. In Korea, the KTX puts Busan 2 h 30 from Seoul and books without difficulty.

**China.** The high-speed network is spectacular — Beijing to Shanghai in 4 h 30 — and cheap for the distance. The trap is elsewhere: **tickets go on sale fifteen days ahead and move fast.** Expecting to find one the day before is the best way to break an itinerary. A passport is required to buy and to board. [The full rule is here](/en/blog/china-train-booking-window).

**Vietnam.** The Reunification line runs 1,726 km down the country. Hanoi to Saigon in one go is 32 h 45 on the SE1 night train: [the fare table is public](/en/blog/hanoi-saigon-train), and almost nobody should do the whole thing. Hanoi to Hue, or Hue to Da Nang in a berth, is excellent — and the Hue–Da Nang section along the Hai Van pass is one of the finest rail journeys in Asia.

**Thailand.** A slow network, but comfortable in a berth. Bangkok to Chiang Mai overnight remains a classic that works. Book first or second-class sleeper, never a seat.

**Laos.** The high-speed Vientiane–Luang Prabang–Boten line has transformed the country: what took ten hours of mountain road takes two. Book ahead; availability is limited.

For overland booking in Southeast Asia, [12Go](https://12go.asia) is the most reliable aggregator, with real timetables and named operators — which local ticket windows do not always provide in English.

## Buses, and how to avoid the bad ones

Buses cover everything the trains do not, at two to three times less. Quality, on the other hand, varies enormously.

**Good signs:** a named, identifiable operator, departure from an official bus station rather than a shopfront agency, and a fare in line with the others. A bus at half the going rate is half the price for a reason.

**The classic trap:** the "VIP bus" sold in tourist districts, which turns out to be an overloaded minibus with a commission stop at a jade shop. Best documented in Cambodia and Laos.

**Vietnamese sleeper buses** deserve a word: the berths are sized for someone 1.65 m tall. Above 1.80 m, the night will be hard.

## Internal flights

Essential in the Philippines and Indonesia, where almost everything is a flight or a ferry. Three rules.

**Three hours minimum between two internal flights.** Delays are the norm, not the exception, and a missed connection across two separate tickets is protected by nobody.

**Cabin baggage is strictly weighed** on Asian low-cost carriers, often at 7 kg. The excess paid at the gate costs three times the excess paid online.

**Book internal flights separately from international ones** only if you leave half a day of margin. Otherwise one ticket, one liability.

## Ferries

In the Philippines, Indonesia and southern Thailand, the ferry is a mode of transport in its own right. Two things to watch: the weather, which cancels without notice, and the state of the boats, which varies a great deal.

**What to check:** that the operator is registered, that the boat is not visibly overloaded, and where the life jackets are. This is not paranoia: incidents on secondary routes are regular, and they are rarely down to weather alone.

## The scooter: the decision with the heaviest consequences

This is the subject on which this guide is least nuanced, because the numbers do not allow it.

Hiring a two-wheeler in Southeast Asia without a valid motorcycle licence and an international driving permit means riding **with no insurance whatsoever**. In an accident, medical costs, repatriation — 20,000 to 80,000 € — and third-party damage fall entirely on you. Hire firms check nothing, which changes nothing about your cover.

If you want to ride, and it is a very good way to travel:

- Get the motorcycle licence before you leave.
- Get the international driving permit, which is free and issued in a few weeks.
- Check that your insurance explicitly covers riding a two-wheeler.
- Wear a full-face helmet, not the building-site helmet the hire firm hands you.
- Never leave your passport as a deposit. A photocopy, or cash.

[The Ha Giang loop](/en/blog/ha-giang-loop-four-days) is the clearest case of all this: four days of mountain road where the right answer, for most people, is to travel as a passenger.

## The apps that genuinely help

One per region, and they replace nearly everything: **Grab** in Southeast Asia, **Gojek** in Indonesia, **DiDi** in China, **Kakao T** in Korea, **GO** in Japan. They remove the negotiation, the language barrier and most taxi scams.

The only prerequisite is a local number, or an account created before departure. [The full list of apps worth installing](/en/blog/travel-apps-for-asia) is short — six, not thirty.
