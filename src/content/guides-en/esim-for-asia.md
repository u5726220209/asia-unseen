Staying connected while travelling went from a problem to a non-problem in five years. The real question today is no longer how to connect but how much to pay in order not to think about it.

## The three options, with their real costs

**The eSIM.** You buy online before departure, scan a QR code, and land connected. Allow 8 to 15 € for 5 to 10 GB in one country, 20 to 35 € for regional multi-country coverage. Pay more attention to the plan than to the brand: from the same provider, unlimited costs four times the 10 GB plan, as [the detailed price comparison](/en/blog/esim-asia-price-comparison) shows.

**The local SIM.** Bought at the airport or in an operator's shop. Two to three times cheaper: in Vietnam or Thailand, 5 to 8 € buys 15 to 30 GB for a month. You also get a local number, which matters more than people expect — Grab, Gojek and most delivery services want a number from the country.

**Your home operator's roaming plan.** The most expensive by a distance. Outside your own region, "world" add-ons run at roughly 10 to 20 € a day or per reduced data bundle. Keep it for trips of two or three days.

## How to decide, in three questions

**How many countries?** One, for more than three weeks: local SIM. Several countries, or under three weeks: regional eSIM.

**Do you need a local number?** If you intend to use Grab or Gojek, order food, or book by phone, yes. Many eSIMs provide data only, with no usable number.

**How much time are you willing to lose on arrival?** Buying a SIM at the airport takes twenty minutes on average, more at Denpasar or Manila at peak hours. The eSIM removes that step.

**The combination that works best:** a small eSIM for the first few days, then a local SIM bought calmly in town once you are settled. You are connected the moment you land, and you pay the local rate for most of the trip.

## The Chinese case, handled separately

China is the only country on this list where the connection choice genuinely changes your trip.

A Chinese local SIM, or an eSIM whose traffic exits through a Chinese server, subjects you to the filtering: Google, Gmail, WhatsApp, Instagram and most Western services are unreachable. An eSIM routed via Hong Kong, by contrast, gets around that filtering in practice.

**What to do before leaving for China:**

1. Choose an eSIM having explicitly checked the routing — good providers state it.
2. Install and **test** a VPN from home, as a safety belt. A VPN downloaded once you are there is a VPN you will not download: the app stores are filtered too.
3. Link a foreign bank card to Alipay or WeChat Pay. In China, mobile payment is no longer a convenience, it is the default infrastructure.

[The full pre-departure sequence](/en/blog/arriving-in-china-preparation) covers all three, with the train booking window.

## The providers, and what separates them

Two models coexist.

[Airalo](https://www.airalo.com) works by data volume: you buy 5, 10 or 20 GB, valid for a period. It is the cheaper model if you use wifi most of the time and mobile data occasionally — maps, messages, bookings.

[Holafly](https://esim.holafly.com) works mainly on unlimited data for a period. More expensive, but relevant if you tether, work remotely or watch video. Check the reduced-speed conditions beyond a daily volume.

In both cases the decisive criterion is **the local operator the eSIM attaches to**. An eSIM riding on a secondary operator will have poor coverage outside cities — which matters in Laos, in the Indonesian islands and in the Philippine provinces.

## The mistakes that cost a day

**Buying the eSIM on arrival.** You need internet to install an eSIM. With no working airport wifi, you are stuck. Buy and install **before you leave**, and activate on landing.

**Forgetting to disable data on your main line.** That is how people come home to a 300 € bill. In the settings, switch off data roaming on the home line explicitly.

**Not checking the carrier lock.** A phone bought on a subsidised contract may refuse a third-party eSIM. Checking the night before departure is too late: unlocking takes a few days.

**Relying on hotel wifi.** Fine in Japan and Korea, erratic elsewhere, and often unusable for a video call.

## How much data you actually need

Based on normal travel use — maps, messaging, social media, a few searches, wifi in the evening:

- **1 to 2 GB a week** on light use, wifi in the evening.
- **3 to 5 GB a week** on normal use, with daily GPS navigation.
- **10 GB or more a week** if you tether, post video or work remotely.

GPS navigation is by far the biggest consumer. Downloading your destination's offline maps before you leave halves or thirds the consumption — and works in areas with no signal, which in Laos or the Visayas happens more often than people expect.
