The market for tourist activities in Asia is enormous and very uneven. The same trip can be sold at 15 € in a side street and 65 € on a platform, and sometimes it is the expensive one that is better run. Here is how to decide.

## What is consistently worth the price

**A good guide on a complex historical site.** Angkor without a guide is beautiful stone. Angkor with someone who knows Khmer iconography is a story. The cost — 30 to 60 € for the day — is the best cultural investment of the trip. The same goes for the imperial tombs at Hue, the Forbidden City, and the temples of Kyoto. [Our Angkor article](/en/blog/angkor-in-the-rainy-season) suggests taking the guide on day two, once you have seen enough to have questions.

**A cookery class, once.** The activity with the best long-term return: you bring home something you will make again. Pick one that starts at the market.

**Diving with a certified centre.** In the Philippines, Indonesia and at Koh Tao, the value is unmatched anywhere in the world. Never economise on this one: the centre's certification and the state of the equipment are worth every euro of difference.

**A trek with a village guide.** In northern Vietnam, in Laos, in northern Thailand. The money goes directly to a local community, and the access is incomparable.

## What costs less on the ground

Day trips, transfers, equipment hire and most guided visits are better arranged the evening before, locally, than online three months out.

**The method:** ask your accommodation. A family guesthouse knows the reliable driver, the boat that does not overload, the guide who genuinely speaks English. That information is worth more than a platform rating, and it is free.

**The exception:** if you are travelling for few days and missing an activity would cost half of one, booking online buys certainty. A platform such as [GetYourGuide](https://www.getyourguide.com) or [Klook](https://www.klook.com) gives free cancellation up to 24 hours before and someone to talk to if it goes wrong — which the seller in the side street does not. On a short trip, that is worth the premium.

## What must be booked ahead

- **Rationed trekking permits** — some national parks cap daily entries.
- **Diving certifications** — small-group places go weeks ahead in high season.
- **Shows and sumo in Japan**, baseball tickets in Korea.
- **Quota-limited temples and gardens** in Japan, some of which take online booking only.
- **Komodo from Labuan Bajo** — to choose the operator, not for the price.

## What to refuse

**Animal attractions.** Elephant rides, tiger photographs, dolphin shows, snake farms. There is no ethical version of these: the animal was broken to make them possible. Serious sanctuaries exist — they offer no contact, no riding and no show, and they say so themselves.

**Orphanage tourism**, in Cambodia particularly. UNICEF has documented for years an industry that separates children from living families to supply paid visits. No orphanage visit belongs in an itinerary.

**The cut-price city tour.** The tuk-tuk offering a tour of town for a token fare takes you to three commission shops. Not dangerous, simply half a day gone.

**Ha Long bay trips sold on the street in Hanoi.** The boat advertised and the boat you board are frequently different. Go through your accommodation or an identifiable operator.

## The four questions to ask before booking

1. **How many people in the group?** A "small group" can mean 8 or 35.
2. **Who is the guide, and what language do they actually speak?** "English speaking" covers a wide spectrum.
3. **What is included?** Entry fees, meals, transport, equipment. That is where the price gap between two identical-looking offers hides.
4. **What happens if the weather turns?** Postponement, refund, or nothing? On anything on the water, that is the decisive question.

## The budget to expect

Over a two- to three-week trip, activities come to roughly 16 % of the on-the-ground budget — but the distribution is very uneven. One or two large items (a trekking permit, a diving certification, a three-day Angkor pass) often account for half the total.

Budget those large items explicitly in [your overall budget](/en/guides/asia-travel-budget) and leave the rest floating. The best activities of a trip to Asia are rarely the ones that were planned.
