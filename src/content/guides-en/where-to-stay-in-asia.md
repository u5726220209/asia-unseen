Choosing where to sleep has more impact on a trip to Asia than choosing the flight or the restaurants. And the most common mistake is not overpaying: it is sleeping in the wrong place.

## The district rule comes before the price rule

A well-placed hotel at 25 € beats an 18 € one forty minutes from the centre. Over a fortnight, the 100 € difference is more than repaid by the journeys avoided — in money and in tiredness.

Three checks before confirming a booking:

**Open the address in a map, not in the platform.** Platforms show a straight-line distance that, in Asia, bears no relation to real travel time. A river, an expressway with no crossing, or the absence of a pavement turns 1.5 km into thirty unpleasant minutes.

**Work out the journey to two places you will genuinely go.** Not "the centre" — a specific station, a specific district.

**Look at the street in immersive view.** Thirty seconds is enough to spot building works, a dual carriageway outside the door, or a lane that looks nothing like the photographs.

The commonest district traps are documented city by city: [which station in Bangkok](/en/blog/where-to-stay-bangkok), [the Kyoto station reflex](/en/blog/where-to-stay-kyoto), [the noise question in Hanoi](/en/blog/where-to-stay-hanoi). None of these areas is bad in itself; all of them get chosen by default, for the wrong reasons.

## When to book, exactly

**The first two or three nights: from home, always.** Arriving after fourteen hours in the air, with no connection and no room, is the best way to overpay for somewhere mediocre. It is the only truly compulsory booking.

**High season or a local festival: all of it, in advance.** Lunar New Year, Songkran, Japan's Golden Week, Chinese Golden Week, Christmas and New Year in Thailand. In those weeks the good places go two to three months ahead and prices double.

**The rest: on the ground, or a few days out.** This is where the quality of a trip is won. You stay three more nights in the village you like; you leave the morning after in the town you do not. That flexibility is worth more than the 10 % an early booking saves.

## Reading reviews properly

The overall score is the least informative number on the page. An 8.4 can cover an excellent hotel with a noisy air conditioner, or a mediocre place with lovely staff.

**What to do:** sort by date, read the last twenty reviews, and look for recurrence. A fault mentioned by three different people in two months is structural. A fault mentioned once a year ago is not.

**The signals that matter in Asia:** sound insulation (the horns start at six), damp and mould in the wet season, whether the air conditioning is reliable, hot water pressure, and wifi quality if you depend on it.

**The signals that do not:** breakfast (disappointing nearly everywhere, and there is better on the street 200 metres away), the decoration, and the "value for money" score, which mostly reflects the expectations of whoever left it.

## The platforms, and how to use them

[Booking.com](https://www.booking.com) remains the reference for coverage of small family-run places and for free cancellation, which is close to universal there. It is also the simplest platform in a dispute.

[Agoda](https://www.agoda.com) has historically been stronger in Asia, sometimes with properties absent elsewhere and lower rates. Beware: the headline price often excludes taxes and fees, which appear at the final step. Always compare the **final price**.

**The reflex that saves 10 to 15 %:** once you have identified a place, look for its own site or contact page. Many small Asian hotels give a discount for booking direct, because they save the platform's commission. A polite message in English is enough. Telling you this earns us nothing — it is simply true.

## The kinds of accommodation that only exist in Asia

**The Japanese business hotel.** A spotless 12 m² room with a prefabricated bathroom, 60 to 80 € in a city centre. The best value in urban Japan, far ahead of the international chains.

**The Vietnamese and Lao homestay.** In a family home, often with a shared meal, 10 to 20 € a night. The best way to genuinely meet people, and the money goes straight to the family. [The Ha Giang loop](/en/blog/ha-giang-loop-four-days) is built on them.

**The Japanese ryokan and the Korean hanok.** Expensive, one or two nights, to be treated as an activity rather than as accommodation.

**The Southeast Asian family guesthouse.** 12 to 25 € a night, usually family-run, with organisational help worth any agency.

## The six commonest booking traps

1. **The superior-room photograph.** Many listings illustrate the base category with pictures of the best room. Check the photo captions.
2. **"Near the beach" across a main road.** Common in Thailand and Indonesia.
3. **Tourist tax not included.** Payable in cash on arrival in several countries.
4. **A passport left as a deposit.** Refuse: a photocopy is enough, and it is what the law provides for in most of the countries concerned.
5. **A property not licensed to take foreign guests**, which is specific to China. Checking before booking avoids being turned away at check-in.
6. **The "free" airport shuttle.** Often a commissioned driver who will take you to two shops first.

## What a good place actually buys you

The best test of accommodation in Asia is not the room: it is the person at the desk. A family who calls you the right driver, tells you which bus to take, warns you there is a festival in the next village, will save you more than any app. That is what the big chains do not provide, and it is exactly what you are looking for.
